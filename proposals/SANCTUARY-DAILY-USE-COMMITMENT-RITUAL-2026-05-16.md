## The Research Lens

The commitment-device literature has two centres of gravity. The first is **stake-and-stick**: StickK and Beeminder put money or social standing on the line, hold a referee, and let loss aversion do the work. The second is **plan-and-cue**: Gollwitzer's implementation intentions ("if situation X, then response Y"), now meta-analysed across 642 tests in 2024 with effect sizes of 0.27–0.66, work by binding behaviour to a perceptual cue rather than to a punishment. The first lane is unavailable to FRQNCY by editorial constraint. The second is wide open and under-used by the wellness category.

What the contemplative traditions add — and what the academic commitment-device literature mostly misses — is that the most binding commitments humans have ever invented are *not* punitive. They are **vow-shaped**. A Buddhist samaya is not a contract with a sanction; it is a bond — what you *do*, not what you *refrain from*. A Quaker testimony is not a rule; it is what one discovers one cannot, in conscience, fail to do. The Stoic hypomnemata are not a journaling assignment; they are the practice itself. Arendt locates the political faculty of promising as one of two human faculties (the other is forgiveness) that make action possible in the first place. David Whyte writes that "all the true vows are secret vows; the ones we speak out loud are the ones we break" — and that the vow worth keeping is the one made "out of the silence" in your own voice.

The Sanctuary is the right surface to host this. It is private (no public-shame surface area). It is daily (the right cadence for an evening-review pattern). It is the user's own page (no comparative leverage). What it currently lacks is a **commitment frame** that gives a returning visitor a reason their nervous system recognises to come back tomorrow.

## Section 1 — The Chief Aim as Vow, Not Goal

The Sanctuary currently asks the user for a Chief Aim (Hill's Definite Chief Aim, read aloud morning and night). The mechanism Hill borrowed — repetition into the subconscious — is sound and well-evidenced. But "Aim" reads as goal language. A goal is a target you either hit or miss; a vow is a *bond you keep or break*. The difference matters because the failure modes are different. A missed goal generates self-blame and avoidance (the StickK-style guilt loop without the financial stake). A broken vow generates re-attunement — the practitioner notices the break, *renews*, and continues. The Buddhist samaya tradition handles slippage through restoration, not punishment. The Quaker tradition handles drift through corporate discernment, not censure.

**Proposal:** Reframe the Chief Aim field as *the Vow*. Same input field. Different verb stack around it. Where the current copy says "write your Chief Aim" or "your purpose," the Sanctuary copy says: *"What is the vow you make from the silence, in your own voice?"* The micro-copy underneath quotes Whyte: *all the true vows are secret vows.* The field is private by default and stays private. The user is not making a promise to FRQNCY. They are making a promise to themselves, witnessed by the page.

This is doing real work. Vow-language activates the right neurology: it is *identity-binding*, not outcome-binding. Arendt's argument is that without promise-keeping we cannot keep our identities — we wander helplessly in the darkness of each contradicting moment. The Sanctuary becomes the place where the user is reminded who they have already said they are.

## Section 2 — The Evening Review as Daily Anchor

Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* are hypomnemata — personal notes, not a book for others. The Stoic discipline was bookended: morning *premeditatio* (rehearse the day's likely difficulties and the response), evening review (Seneca: "I examine my entire day and go back over what I've done and said, hiding nothing from myself"). The discipline is *both ends*, and the evening end is the load-bearing one. The morning sets the intention; the evening is where the practitioner discovers whether the intention met the day.

The commitment-device research independently arrives at the same point through a different door. Gollwitzer's implementation intentions are most effective when *rehearsed*, when the cue is concrete, and when the practitioner is highly motivated. Beeminder's "akrasia horizon" — the 7-day delay on changing your own goal — works because it forces decisions to be made *in advance of the impulse*, embedding them in reflective rather than impulsive cognition. The evening review is the same mechanism running daily: a small reflective surface that the next morning's impulse cannot reach.

**Proposal:** The Sanctuary's daily-return engine is an evening review, not a morning planner. Three prompts, each one line:

1. *Where did I keep the vow today?* (the win — named, not measured)
2. *Where did I miss it?* (the slip — described, not graded)
3. *What is the cue for tomorrow?* (the if-then — Gollwitzer's specific format: "if X situation, then Y response")

Three fields. No streak counter. No score. The user returns tomorrow because tomorrow's morning prompt is the cue *they themselves wrote last night*. The Sanctuary is a mirror of their own commitment — Arendt's "presence and acting of others" replaced by the presence of their prior self.

## Section 3 — Rituals the Sanctuary Can Host Without Becoming Churchy

The risk in this design space is that the Sanctuary drifts into devotional-tech kitsch — incense fonts, candle gifs, "sacred" everything. FRQNCY's voice rules out spiritual cliché and over-translation. The rituals that work are the ones that name themselves plainly and *do something*. Here is what survives the voice test:

- **The renewal, not the streak.** When a user misses a day, the Sanctuary does not show a broken-chain icon. It shows a single line: *the vow is renewed when you return.* Buddhist samaya practice handles slippage through restoration prayers — the failure mode is built into the practice, not penalised by it. This is also Whyte's frame: the making and breaking of a promise involve the same combination of absolute presence and radical letting go.
- **The witness, not the audience.** The Sanctuary is a witness surface, not a broadcast surface. The user's vow, evening review, and cues are visible only to them. This is the Quaker move — testimony as inward witness made outward only through one's own life, not through performance.
- **The weekly meeting with self.** Once a week — Quaker yearly-meeting cadence compressed — the Sanctuary surfaces a single slow-read view: the week's seven vow-renewals, the seven cues, the seven slips. Not a dashboard. A page that takes five minutes to read. Corporate discernment, alone.
- **The seasonal vow rewrite.** Every quarter the Sanctuary offers the user a chance to *rewrite the vow*. Not a forced update — a held door. Whyte's "the necessary art of breaking outworn, misguided, or out-of-season bonds" is built into the design. A vow held past its season becomes a constraint; a vow renewed in season stays alive.

None of these require liturgy, candles, or sacred-mode UI. They require precise verbs and the absence of gamification.

## Section 4 — Seven Concrete Proposals

1. **Rename the Chief Aim field to "the Vow,"** with micro-copy quoting Whyte and a single-line prompt: *what is the vow you make from the silence, in your own voice?* Keep the field private by default. Keep the morning/evening read-aloud mechanic from Hill — it is the evidence-backed part — but the verb is now *renew*, not *recite*.
2. **Replace any streak counter with a "renewal" affordance.** A missed day surfaces a single line, *the vow is renewed when you return*, and a one-tap renewal that re-reads the vow back to the user. No broken-chain icon. No "you lost your streak." The samaya restoration logic, ported.
3. **Build the three-line evening review as the Sanctuary's daily-return engine.** Three prompts: kept, missed, cue-for-tomorrow. The cue field enforces Gollwitzer's if-then format with a placeholder ("if it is 6am and I am in the kitchen, then I sit for ten minutes"). The morning prompt is the cue *the user themselves wrote*. Tomorrow's friction is reduced to recognising one's own handwriting.
4. **Add a weekly review page** — seven-vow-renewals slow-read, no charts, no totals. Surfaces on Sundays. The yearly-meeting cadence compressed into a week, the Quaker corporate-discernment move made solo.
5. **Add a seasonal "rewrite the vow" prompt** — every 90 days the Sanctuary asks once: *is this still the vow?* Whyte's out-of-season-bond release valve, built into the cadence. No prompt fatigue: it is asked four times a year.
6. **Use the akrasia-horizon mechanic for vow changes**, but only one direction. Making the vow *more demanding* takes effect immediately. Making it *less demanding* — or deleting it — takes seven days. This is Beeminder's mechanic adapted to a non-financial context: it protects the user from their own akratic moments without ever taking their money. It is also philosophically consistent with FRQNCY's posture: the platform is on the side of the user's deeper commitment, not their momentary mood.
7. **Make the vow witness-able by one specific person, never by a crowd.** If the user wants accountability, they can share their vow with exactly one named person (a teacher, partner, sibling — not a "buddy" assigned by the platform, not a leaderboard cohort). This is Arendt's plurality requirement honoured in its smallest viable form: a promise is real because one other person knows it exists, not because a network is watching.

---

## What This Costs To Build

The hard parts are copy, not engineering. The three-line evening review is a form. The renewal logic is a state machine with one state. The weekly review is a query over the last seven entries. The akrasia-horizon-for-vow-changes is a 7-day timestamp check. The seasonal rewrite is a cron prompt. The voice work — making sure none of this reads as churchy or gamified — is the actual project, and it lives in the playbook already.

The Sanctuary becomes the place a user returns to not because the platform extracts a stake or a shame, but because they made a vow there, in their own voice, that they cannot quite hear anywhere else.
