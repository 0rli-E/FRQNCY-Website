Wellbeing

The body knows how. Wellbeing is what happens when you stop interfering. Modern medicine treats the breakdown. The deeper work is the conditions that prevent the breakdown in the first place.

Natural forces within us are the true healers of disease. Hippocrates
return

The body has been doing this for hundreds of millions of years. Every time you cut yourself, catch a cold, sprain a wrist, lose sleep — a system older than written language repairs the damage. The repair runs whether you understand it or not. The work, mostly, is to give it the conditions it needs and to stop sabotaging it.

What follows is what FRQNCY treats as the floor — six pillars the body cannot do without, the interfering inputs to remove, and the lineages worth listening to when the floor is solid and you want to go deeper.

Let food be thy medicine and medicine be thy food. Hippocrates · c. 400 BCE
The body is not broken. The conditions it lives in are.
I · The six pillars

Six conditions the body cannot do without.

Almost every modern chronic condition has a root in one or more of these going wrong for long enough. The interventions are not exotic. The discipline is in the consistency.

i

Sleep

Seven to nine hours, dark room, cool, no phone within reach. The single highest-leverage wellbeing input. Most chronic disease compounds out of chronic sleep deprivation. The first thing to fix; the hardest to defend.

Glymphatic clearance · hormonal reset · memory consolidation
ii

Food

Real food. Recognisable as plant or animal. Cooked at home most of the time. Less ultra-processed packaging. Animal protein for satiety; plants for fibre and micronutrients; fat for the brain. The specifics matter less than the consistency of returning to whole-food defaults.

Insulin signal · gut microbiome · mitochondrial fuel
iii

Movement

Walk daily. Strength-train twice a week. Sprint occasionally. The body that does not move atrophies — bones, muscles, mitochondria, mood. A long unbroken walking habit is more health-protective than nearly any exercise programme that sometimes happens.

Mitochondrial density · insulin sensitivity · neurogenesis
iv

Breath

Through the nose, slow, into the belly. Most adults are over-breathing — chronic stress drives chest-breathing through an open mouth, which down-regulates everything from immune function to sleep quality. Breath is the only autonomic system you can directly steer; use it.

Vagal tone · CO₂ tolerance · nervous-system regulation
v

Sun & light

Twenty minutes of morning sun on the skin, no sunglasses. Sunset light on the eyes. Avoid bright artificial light after dark. Circadian rhythm is the master regulator of nearly every other system in the body; light is what sets it.

Vitamin D · circadian alignment · mood regulation
vi

Connection

Relationships you actually look forward to. Touch you actually feel safe in. Community you actually belong to. The Harvard Adult Development Study followed people for 80 years and found the strongest single predictor of long-term wellbeing was the quality of close relationships. Loneliness compounds like compound interest, in reverse.

HRV · oxytocin · longevity
II · Stop interfering

What you remove matters more than what you add.

The supplement industry sells the addition story — drink this, take this, fix this. The deeper work is usually subtraction. The body is already trying to repair. Most of what's in the way is something you can stop putting in.

The body is not broken. The signals it is receiving are. after Bruce Lipton

The shortlist of inputs to subtract first, in roughly priority order:

  1. Ultra-processed food. The single largest input shift in modern diets. Hydrogenated oils, seed oils, refined sugars, artificial dyes, preservative cocktails. Replacing 80% of these with home-cooked whole food usually does more than any supplement protocol.
  2. Chronic stress. The body cannot tell a deadline apart from a predator. Persistent cortisol elevation degrades almost every system. The intervention is not "manage stress" — it is removing the source where possible, and learning vagal regulation where not.
  3. Plastics and chemical exposure. Microplastics in food and water, fragrance chemicals in skin contact, fluorinated compounds in cookware, pesticide residues, off-gassing from new furniture. Each one is small. The cumulative load is not. See Detox.
  4. Pharmaceutical over-reliance. Some medications are the difference between life and death. Some are masking a problem the body would resolve faster if given conditions. The honest evaluation requires a doctor who is willing to remove drugs as well as add them. Functional medicine practitioners tend to ask both questions; allopathic models often only ask one.
  5. Information overload. Phones in the bedroom. Notifications all day. Twenty-four-hour news cycles. The nervous system was built to encounter a few sources of novelty per day; we encounter several thousand. The body reads it as low-grade emergency for years on end.
Genes do not determine our destiny. Our perceptions of the environment do. Bruce Lipton · The Biology of Belief
III · Two medicines

Conventional & functional.

There is more than one model of medicine. Both are useful. Knowing which one fits which situation is part of becoming a competent operator on your own behalf.

Conventional / allopathic

Treats the breakdown.

Diagnostic. Pharmaceutical. Surgical. Specialty-fragmented. Excellent at acute crisis — a trauma, an infection, a fracture, an emergency. Less suited to chronic conditions where the cause is environmental, behavioural, or systemic. Insurance reimburses interventions, not prevention; the model bends accordingly.

Functional & integrative

Treats the conditions.

Root-cause oriented. Considers food, sleep, environment, relationships, mental and emotional state as primary inputs. More expensive (rarely insurance-covered), often slower, often more effective for chronic disease. Practitioners include MDs, naturopaths, traditional Chinese medicine doctors, ayurvedic physicians. Quality varies; vet carefully.

The right move is rarely one or the other. Acute crisis: conventional. Chronic condition with a thousand small contributing factors: functional. Most serious chronic-illness journeys end up using both, and the patient who's done their own research tends to get better outcomes than the patient who hasn't.

IV · Trauma in the body

The body keeps the score.

For most of the twentieth century, mind and body were treated as separate systems. The work of Bessel van der Kolk, Gabor Maté, Peter Levine, Stephen Porges, and many others has now overwhelmingly demonstrated that they are not. Stored psychological stress shows up as physical disease. Repressed emotion shows up as autoimmunity, inflammation, chronic pain, addiction. The body remembers what the mind has filed away.

Trauma is not what happens to you. It is what happens inside you as a result of what happened to you. Gabor Maté · The Myth of Normal

This changes the picture of wellbeing in two important ways. First, you cannot fully resolve a chronic condition while leaving its emotional substrate untouched. Anti-inflammatory diets that ignore underlying nervous-system dysregulation tend to plateau. Second, the modalities that move stored trauma — somatic experiencing, EMDR, breathwork, psychedelic-assisted therapy where legal, family-systems work — are not luxuries. For chronic-disease patients they are often more decisive than the next supplement.

Joe Dispenza's broader claim — that sustained changes in thought and emotional state produce measurable changes in the body — is more contested in mainstream science but consistent with the trauma-and-healing literature when properly bounded. Take the most ambitious framings with appropriate scepticism. Take the underlying observation seriously.

A practice

Five small things, repeated.

The pillars are obvious. The practice is the discipline of returning to them when life pulls you off. Five small things that change what wellbeing does in a life:

i
Phone outside the bedroom.

The single most leveraged change available to most adults. Better sleep. Less morning anxiety. Recovered evenings. A $10 alarm clock makes it possible. Most people resist this for a week and then never go back.

ii
Twenty minutes of morning sun, no sunglasses.

Within an hour of waking. Light on the skin and through the eyes. Sets circadian rhythm, primes vitamin D, regulates mood. Free, fast, almost unfair in how much it changes how the day feels.

iii
Walk an hour a day.

Cumulatively, in any combination. Outside if possible, with another person if available. Walking is the most-protective single behaviour in longitudinal health studies. The bar is low; the compounding is enormous.

iv
Cook one meal a day from real ingredients.

Not perfectly, not gourmet — just real. Vegetables, eggs, meat, fish, fruit, rice, beans, butter, olive oil, salt. The skill compounds. The supply line shifts. The relationship to food rebuilds. Six months of this changes what the body wants.

v
Find one person you can be honest with.

Not a therapist necessarily. Could be a friend, partner, sibling, mentor. The Harvard 80-year study's headline result. The most under-priced wellbeing intervention available. Depression and chronic stress drop sharply when one such relationship is in place; they compound brutally without it.

People, books, and tools we actually use.

Andrew Huberman

Stanford neuroscientist whose podcast turns peer-reviewed sleep, stress, and metabolism research into actionable protocols. Long-form, technical, honest about what the evidence does and doesn't say. The cleanest entry-point to evidence-based wellbeing on the internet.

The four horsemen of chronic disease, the five tactical domains (exercise, nutrition, sleep, emotional health, exogenous molecules), the uncomfortable trade-offs. If you read one wellbeing book this year, this one.

The Body Keeps the Score

Bessel van der Kolk, 2014. The canonical popular work on how trauma lives in the body and what reliably moves it. The Biology of Belief

Bruce Lipton, 2005. Cell biologist's account of epigenetics — how the cellular environment, not the gene alone, controls expression. Some of the popular framings outrun the data; the underlying observation is now mainstream science. Worth reading critically.

Person

Wim Hof

The Iceman. Cold exposure plus structured breathwork as a self-administered protocol for stress resilience and immune function. Some of the wilder claims are unsupported; controlled trials of the basic method (cold + breath) consistently show measurable physiological effects. A fixture of the modern practice canon.

Tool

Oura Ring · Whoop · Apple Watch

Wearable sleep and recovery tracking. The specific device matters less than the practice of attending to the data. Most people who wear one for six months end up with meaningfully better sleep just from the feedback loop. Oura is the most accurate for sleep stages; Whoop for HRV; Apple Watch is the convenient compromise.
