# Social Content — 30-Day Calendar

**Purpose.** A complete 30-day cadence Orlando can execute by literal copy-paste. Three X posts per week, two LinkedIn posts per week, drawing material from FRQNCY's existing content (146 topic pages, 766+ resources, the editorial-standards page, the network-state reference essay, the founder profile). No new claims invented; every post points at something already on the site.

**Cadence target.**
- X / Twitter: 3 posts per week × 4.4 weeks = **13 X posts over 30 days**
- LinkedIn: 2 posts per week × 4.4 weeks = **9 LinkedIn posts over 30 days**
- Total: **22 scheduled posts** (one per working day across 30 calendar days)

**Working days.** Mon–Fri only. Weekends are off (Day 6, 7, 13, 14, 20, 21, 27, 28 — these are deliberately blank to keep the cadence sustainable). 30 calendar days starting on a Monday gives 22 working days.

**Suggested time slots.**
- X morning: 8:30–10:00 AM local (highest engagement for FRQNCY's audience — US East waking, Europe mid-morning)
- X afternoon: 2:00–3:30 PM local (secondary slot when morning is missed)
- LinkedIn: 8:00–10:00 AM Tue/Wed/Thu (peak B2B engagement)

**Format mix across 30 days.**
- Topic spotlights: 8 (≥30% target hit — 36%)
- Resource picks: 5 (≥20% — 23%)
- Manifesto lines: 4 (~18%)
- Threads: 3 (≥10% — 14%; each is 6–7 tweets)
- Reply-bait questions (honest, not bait): 2 (~9%)
- Cross-promote: 2 (~9% — keyed to actual ships, swap if nothing ships)

**Voice gate before every post.** Present-tense, declarative, conviction not hype. No banned phrases (unlock, leverage, synergy, 10x, circle back, low-hanging fruit, best practices, actionable insights). No engagement bait, no outcome promises, no mass-tagging. Always qualify "FRQNCY Network" when the context is brand-ambiguous (X bios + first tweets of a thread).

**Char counts.** Every X post is annotated with character count including spaces. Links count as 23 chars on X. LinkedIn posts use the 3000-char practical max.

## Sequencing logic — why Day 1 is what it is

The first ten days establish voice and surface (what FRQNCY *is*) so a follower arriving on Day 11 has enough context to read the rest as a coherent project. The middle ten days deepen — threads, founder lessons, resource picks. The last ten days widen — opinion posts, cross-promotion, the reading-list thread as the anchor piece.

The arc on rough outline:
- **Days 1–7 (Week 1, foundation):** define the project, declare the editorial standards, surface the explore page. A new follower learns what FRQNCY Network is.
- **Days 8–14 (Week 2, depth):** thread on the taxonomy, two LinkedIn lesson posts, a manifesto line about curation, the first long-form thread.
- **Days 15–21 (Week 3, voice + opinion):** the curation-moat thread, opinion takes on the attention economy, founder reflection on building 146 topics.
- **Days 22–30 (Week 4–5, anchor pieces):** the network-state reading list thread (highest leverage post in the calendar), the manifestation deep-dive thread, two LinkedIn essays, a cross-promote of the reference essay.

A new follower hitting any single day in this arc gets: a topic, a standard, or a question that points back at the project. No day stands alone as pitch.

I built FRQNCY Network around a single editorial standard, and most of the early decisions came down to enforcing it.

The standard is: a pick must be personally vetted. Someone in the picking body has read the book, taken the course, used the product, met the person. "I heard it's good" doesn't qualify.

That sounds obvious. In practice it kills more curation ideas than anything else.

It killed the affiliate revenue model — affiliate links create a tiny pressure to pick what pays, and the standard doesn't allow tiny pressures. It killed the "expand to 500 topics this year" idea, because the picks couldn't keep up. It killed sponsored placements as a category. If a piece was paid for, it isn't editorial — and FRQNCY doesn't publish it. There's no in-between.

What's left is a slower thing. 146 topics. 766 resources, growing by tens per month, not hundreds. Every entry traceable to a named person who vouched.

I expected the constraint to feel limiting. It hasn't. It's the only standard that protects what FRQNCY is actually selling — trust — from what would otherwise eat it.

The full editorial standards are public: frqncy.network/editorial-standards/

The most useful book I've read for thinking about institutions in 2026 was written by an anthropologist and an archaeologist about humans 30,000 years ago.

#### **Day 12 — Friday** · LinkedIn · Lesson-from-shipping
**Time:** 9:00 AM
**Source:** `frqncy.network/about` and the internal harness work

```
A lesson from building a 146-topic library with a small team: the architecture is unglamorous, and that's the point.

Under FRQNCY's topic pages is a harness — a working agent loop that audits, edits, and proposes changes to the network without ever touching the curatorial judgment. Generators write the page HTML. SEO agents check the schema. A reference-essay agent drafts long-form pieces from the existing graph. An MCP server exposes the 146 topics and 766 resources to any compatible LLM.

The rule is one sentence: agents never make picks.

Picks are personally vetted, by name, on the record. Agents handle scale — keeping the chrome consistent across hundreds of pages, catching broken links, drafting the SEO surfaces. Humans handle taste. The two layers stay separate by design.

That separation is what makes the editorial honest. The moment an agent is allowed to recommend a book, the standard for what counts as a recommendation collapses to whatever the model was trained on. We don't ship a library trained on the average internet.

Trace-as-memory is the other invariant: every change an agent makes leaves a run log. The logs are public-internal in audits/seo/runs/. The reasoning is reconstructable from them.

The point isn't that the harness is impressive. It's that the harness lets a small team maintain a wide library at a quality the big sites can't reach — because the big sites optimise for scale, and we optimise for the pick.

The project: frqncy.network/about
```

#### **Day 16 — Tuesday** · LinkedIn · Industry-take
**Time:** 8:30 AM
**Source:** `frqncy.network/editorial-standards/` + Thread Bank #4 framing

```
AI summaries are going to make curation more valuable, not less.

The standard objection is the opposite — that when every page can be paraphrased instantly, there's no reason to read any of them, and therefore no reason to curate any of them. I think this gets the economics backwards.

If everything can be paraphrased, the only premium left is the choice of what to paraphrase. The shelf of books is the moat. Not the shelf — the standard for putting a book on it.

Three observations from running a curated topic graph through this transition:

1. Algorithmic feeds answer the question "what did most people click." That's a different question from "what should one person read next." Most public libraries answer the first better than the second. The second is where editors live.

2. A topic graph differs from a feed in one structural way: it knows how its entries relate. FRQNCY's Meditation page knows it sits next to Breathwork, Yoga, Consciousness, and the books of Jon Kabat-Zinn. That structure is curatorial, not algorithmic. An LLM grounding on it inherits the structure.

3. The pages an LLM cites are the pages a serious editor wrote. The substrate is changing — text moves through models now, not through search results — but the standard for what to write hasn't changed. Conviction. Receipts. No paid placement.

The moat compounds. Each good pick is permission for the next one. Each bad pick is permission revoked.

FRQNCY's full editorial standards: frqncy.network/editorial-standards/
```

The hardest thing about building a 146-topic library wasn't the writing. It was deciding what didn't belong.

When you start curating a field, the temptation is to include everything that's adjacent. Quantum physics belongs, so quantum biology belongs, so structured water belongs, so… and four hops in you're hosting a topic page on a thing nobody on the team has personally vetted, which is the bright line we said we wouldn't cross.

The rule we settled on after the first cull: 146 topics is a constraint, not a budget. Each topic earns its slot by being something a serious reader has to know. If we can't write a one-sentence answer to "why this topic and not five neighbouring ones," it doesn't go on.

The omissions turn out to be more legible than the inclusions. Readers can tell when a list has been pruned. They can also tell when a list was written by someone who didn't know enough to cut anything.

The shorter shelf is the better one. Three years from now, FRQNCY may have 200 topics or it may still have 146. The number is downstream of the standard, not the goal.

If you're building a curated thing in any field — books, products, talent, courses, anything — the question that protects you is: *what would you cut today if you had to cut ten percent.* If you don't have an answer, the curation is decorative.

The project: frqncy.network/about

### Week 5 · Days 29–30 · Close

#### **Day 29 — Monday** · LinkedIn · Industry-take
**Time:** 8:30 AM
**Source:** Founder voice + `frqncy.network/editorial-standards/` + memory file

```
A pattern I keep seeing in 2026: founders building curation products as if curation were a one-time act.

It isn't. The picks are not the work — *retiring* the picks is the work. A library that never retires a recommendation is, in practice, a library that has stopped paying attention.

FRQNCY's editorial standards have an explicit retirement rule. A pick is retired when the reason for picking no longer holds — the teacher reframes their work in a way that conflicts with the values, the product is reformulated, the org is bought, new evidence undermines the original vouch. Retirement is not the same as removal. The entity stays as a directory entry where appropriate; only the endorsement is cleared. We keep the history; we don't keep the endorsement.

The reason that rule matters is incentive. Without a retirement protocol, curation drifts toward what pays — toward the picks that paid off, away from the picks that aged badly. The only honest library is one that can publicly say "we picked this; we don't anymore; here's when and why."

It also turns out to be the cheapest trust signal a curated platform can ship. The retirement notice is a one-line edit. The willingness to ship it is the whole product.

If you're building anything that endorses third parties — a fund, a marketplace, a media outlet, a recommendation engine — the question is what your retirement protocol looks like.

FRQNCY's: frqncy.network/editorial-standards/
```

## Substitution rules

If a scheduled day falls on a public holiday, a personal off-day, or a moment where the recommended copy doesn't fit the news cycle:

- **Skip days 6, 7, 13, 14, 20, 21, 27, 28** are the natural rest days; never schedule there.
- **Substitute any X slot** with a line from `SOCIAL-CONTENT-MANIFESTO-LINES.md`. The lines are all ≤280 chars and pre-cleared for voice.
- **Substitute any LinkedIn slot** with a re-paste of a previous LinkedIn post + a one-paragraph update (acceptable LinkedIn pattern; the algorithm reads it as new).
- **Defer a thread** to the next working day if the morning isn't quiet enough to attend to replies — threads with no founder presence in the first three hours under-perform structurally.

## What this calendar deliberately does not include

- **Engagement-bait posts.** No "drop a 🔥 if you agree", no "tag someone who needs this".
- **Mass tagging.** Tag only when substantively relevant (e.g. quote-tweeting a specific essay that names FRQNCY).
- **Outcome promises.** No "this will change how you read".
- **Pure pitch posts.** Every post is voice-driven editorial. The link is permission for the reader; the post earns it.
- **Hashtag-stuffing.** Hashtags appear at most once or twice across 30 days (e.g. on the network-state thread, optionally `#NetworkState` once — Orlando's call).
- **Replies to brand collisions.** If FRQNCY Media or FMG mentions break through search, log them per `MENTION-MONITORING.md` and don't engage publicly.
