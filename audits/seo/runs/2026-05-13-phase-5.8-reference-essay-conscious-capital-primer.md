# Conscious capital — a primer with sources

*A working canon for the question that almost every serious investor under fifty is now asking — what does it look like to deploy capital so that the world it produces is one you would want to live in, and what is the actual body of work behind the answer.*

Edited by [Orlando Eisenreich](https://frqncy.network/people/orlando/) · Standards: [FRQNCY Editorial](https://frqncy.network/editorial-standards/) · First published 2026-08 (Q3 slot).

---

There is a phrase that has become difficult to use seriously and harder still to use precisely: *conscious capital*. Between the slogan and the substrate sits a real body of work — older than the term, longer than any of its current spokespeople have been alive — that takes the question of how money is deployed as a question about how civilisation gets made. This essay is the working map of that body, meant to be the page a serious reader finds first, links to last, and either argues with or extends.

Conscious capital is not a fund category, a certification, or an asset class. It is a stance: the people who hold capital are responsible for the world that capital builds, and the inherited fiduciary frame — maximise risk-adjusted return for the holder of legal title, full stop — is incomplete enough that practising it as if complete is itself a moral position. From that stance the rest unfolds: longer time horizons because compounding requires intact systems, multiple stakeholders because no business is durable inside a brittle society, honest accounting of externalities because what is excluded from the books does not stop being real, and patience with non-financial returns because the most valuable outputs of capital — soil, trust, beauty, freedom — are not denominated in dollars and never will be.

The stance has many adjacent siblings, which is part of what makes the term contested.

## What conscious capital is not — the family of adjacent terms

Before the lineage there has to be a clearing of the ground, because the words are crowded.

**ESG** (environmental, social, governance) is an *investment screen* — metrics applied to public equities so institutional capital can claim alignment without changing strategy. By 2025 the critique was loud enough to come from inside the house: gameable screens, ratings agencies in directional disagreement, top-rated names that remained extractive in ways no rating captured. ESG is a tool for marking up an existing strategy, not a worldview.

**Impact investing** is the older and more honest sibling — investments made with the explicit dual intent of financial return and measurable social or environmental benefit. The term was coined at the Rockefeller Foundation's 2007 Bellagio convening; the field is now organised under the [Global Impact Investing Network](https://frqncy.network/orgs/global-impact-investing-network/), whose IRIS+ taxonomy is the closest thing to a shared accounting standard. Impact is the practice; conscious capital is the frame that makes the practice make sense.

**Sustainable investing** is the broadest and loosest — negative screens (no tobacco, no weapons, no fossil fuels), thematic funds (clean energy, water, climate transition), or the maximalist version where the portfolio is built to keep planetary systems inside their envelope. Useful as a search term, less useful as a discipline, because almost every fund manager can claim it without lying.

**Regenerative finance** — sometimes ReFi when it travels in crypto circles — is the most ambitious. The aim is not to do less harm but to *put back more than you take*: capital deployed so the soil, the watershed, the community, and the institution are demonstrably healthier at the end of the cycle than the beginning. The serious version inherits from regenerative agriculture, ecological economics, and Indigenous land stewardship — see [Carol Sanford](https://frqncy.network/people/carol-sanford/) on regenerative business, the [Savory Institute](https://frqncy.network/orgs/savory-institute/) on regenerative grazing, and [Janine Benyus](https://frqncy.network/people/janine-benyus/)'s work via the [Biomimicry Institute](https://frqncy.network/orgs/biomimicry-institute/) for the biological design grammar behind it.

**Conscious capitalism** — singular, capital C — is the 2013-era business movement associated with [John Mackey](https://www.consciouscapitalism.org/) (Whole Foods) and Raj Sisodia, whose [Conscious Capitalism](https://frqncy.network/orgs/conscious-capitalism/) organisation is its institutional home. Its four tenets — higher purpose, stakeholder orientation, conscious leadership, conscious culture — describe how a *business* should be run. Conscious capital is upstream: how the people who fund those businesses should think. Where conscious capitalism gives the operating handbook, conscious capital gives the question that comes before it: what is the money for, and what does it owe the world that produced it.

## The intellectual lineage — where the field came from

The current vocabulary is recent. The substrate is centuries old.

The oldest layer is religious. The Quaker capital tradition — the Cadburys, Rowntrees, Frys, Lloyds, and Barclays of nineteenth-century Britain — built businesses on the conviction that profit was a test of useful work and that worker, community, and customer had standing in the firm equal to the owner; the same testimonies of integrity, simplicity, and equality governed the meeting and the firm. Mondragón, the Basque industrial cooperative founded by Father José María Arizmendiarrieta in 1956, is the other landmark — eighty thousand worker-owners structured so capital serves labour rather than the reverse. Both remain working examples; neither has been absorbed by the present-day movement's vocabulary.

The economic-philosophy layer begins with John Ruskin's [*Unto This Last*](https://frqncy.network/books/unto-this-last/), 1860 — political economy had to account for life, beauty, and moral cost or it was not in fact an economy. Gandhi cited it as one of the three books that most influenced him. E. F. Schumacher's *Small is Beautiful* (1973) introduced "Buddhist economics" to the English-speaking world and argued that scale, technology, and ownership were moral questions economists had stopped asking. Wendell Berry's *The Unsettling of America* (1977) extended the critique into agriculture and place.

The systems layer arrived with Donella Meadows and the Limits to Growth team in 1972 and was completed in [*Thinking in Systems*](https://frqncy.network/books/thinking-in-systems/), [Meadows](https://frqncy.network/people/donella-meadows/)'s posthumous primer — the standard reference for anyone reasoning about feedback loops, intervention points, and the gap between an institution's intent and its emergent behaviour. The conscious-capital reader who has not internalised Meadows on the bathtub — flows compound into stocks, and stocks are where the consequences live — will keep making first-order mistakes. Pair with [Peter Senge](https://frqncy.network/people/peter-senge/)'s *The Fifth Discipline* (1990) for the organisational application.

The ecological-economics layer is shorter and more recent. [*Doughnut Economics*](https://frqncy.network/books/doughnut-economics/) by [Kate Raworth](https://frqncy.network/people/kate-raworth/) (2017) is the cleanest public synthesis — the human economy bounded above by planetary limits and below by social foundations, and the work of policy and capital to keep activity inside the ring. The Amsterdam, Brussels, and Copenhagen pilots that have adopted the framework as a city operating doctrine [verify cities as of 2026] are the empirical test. Read Raworth alongside Tim Jackson's *Prosperity Without Growth* (2009/2017), Herman Daly's steady-state work, and [Mariana Mazzucato](https://frqncy.network/books/the-value-of-everything/)'s [*The Value of Everything*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-value-of-everything/) (2018) — the parallel argument about what current value-measurement gets wrong, especially the distinction between value creation and value extraction.

The corporate-form layer is the practitioner branch. [B Lab](https://frqncy.network/orgs/b-lab/), founded in 2006, codified the [B Corp certification](https://frqncy.network/orgs/b-corp-certification/) — an audited standard requiring companies to consider workers, customers, community, and environment in their governing documents, with re-certification every three years against a public scorecard. Over nine thousand certified B Corps now operate across ninety-plus countries [verify exact 2026 numbers], and the benefit corporation legal form has been adopted in most US states. Marjorie Kelly's *Owning Our Future* (2012) and [Frederic Laloux](https://frqncy.network/people/frederic-laloux/)'s [*Reinventing Organizations*](https://frqncy.network/books/reinventing-organizations/) (2014) are the literature of new ownership forms and post-hierarchical operating models that sit beside the certification work.

The cultural and contemplative layer is the most often skipped and the most necessary. [*The Diamond Cutter*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-diamond-cutter/) — by [Geshe Michael Roach](https://frqncy.network/people/geshe-michael-roach/), the Buddhist monk who built Andin International into a quarter-billion-dollar diamond business while teaching Tibetan epistemology — is the clearest accessible bridge between contemplative practice and commercial conduct; pair with [*Karmic Management*](https://frqncy.network/books/karmic-management/). Charles Eisenstein's *Sacred Economics* (2011) states the same conviction in a Western poetic lineage — money was originally an instrument of relationship, and the question of where we lost track of that is working diagnostic, not historical curiosity.

These six layers — religious, philosophical, systems, ecological, corporate-form, contemplative — are not a chronology; they run at once. The practitioner who works only in one of them is making the field smaller than it is.

## The structure of conscious capital — what actually makes it work

The literature is rich; the mechanics are surprisingly few. Stripped to the studs, conscious capital differs from default capital along five axes — ownership, time, metric, fiduciary scope, and disclosure. A given operator does not need to perfect all five; they need to be honest about which they are working on.

**Ownership.** Default capital is shareholder-owned, shareholders unaccountable to the people the business affects. Conscious capital multiplies the owners — employee stock ownership plans, cooperatives (Mondragón industrial, Cleveland Evergreen community), and now the perpetual purpose trust, where the company is owned by a trust whose only beneficiary is its declared purpose. Yvon Chouinard's 2022 transfer of [Patagonia](https://frqncy.network/books/patagonia/) into the Patagonia Purpose Trust is the highest-profile case; [Chouinard](https://frqncy.network/people/yvon-chouinard/)'s [*Let My People Go Surfing*](https://frqncy.network/books/let-my-people-go-surfing/) remains the cleanest first-person account.

**Time horizon.** Default capital chases quarterly returns because the market measures quarterly. Conscious capital lengthens the horizon because the systems being built — soil regeneration, trust networks, watershed restoration, durable institutions — do not return quarterly. Patient, evergreen, perpetual, fifty-year: the vocabulary varies, the structural move is the same — fund term and exit pressure matched to the rhythm of the underlying asset. Joel Solomon's *The Clean Money Revolution* (2017) is the practitioner statement from a generation that has been doing this since the 1990s.

**Metric.** Default capital reports financial return. Conscious capital reports financial return alongside whatever else it is responsible for, and the serious work is on the *non-financial* axis. The IRIS+ taxonomy at the [Global Impact Investing Network](https://frqncy.network/orgs/global-impact-investing-network/) is the most-adopted reference. Jed Emerson's "blended value" framework (2003 paper, 2018 book *The Purpose of Capital*) argues that financial and social returns are a single ledger. [Aaron Hurst](https://frqncy.network/people/aaron-hurst/)'s [*The Purpose Economy*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-purpose-economy/) sits on the demand side, mapping what workers and consumers will pay for once "purpose" stops being a marketing affect.

**Fiduciary scope.** Default capital says the manager's duty runs to the beneficiary and nothing else. Conscious capital reads that duty as conditional on what the beneficiary actually wants — and notes, with evidence, that most beneficiaries want returns *and* a habitable planet and a society they recognise. The legal scholarship on whether ESG and impact considerations are consistent with — or required by — fiduciary duty has expanded enormously since 2018; the Principles for Responsible Investment's "Fiduciary Duty in the 21st Century" reports are the standard reference. [verify most recent edition as of 2026.]

**Disclosure.** Default capital discloses the minimum required. Conscious capital discloses, in plain language, what it owns, what those holdings actually do, and how it knows. The B Corp Impact Assessment is one model; the [Ellen MacArthur Foundation](https://frqncy.network/orgs/ellen-macarthur-foundation/)'s circular-economy reporting is another. An unauditable claim of consciousness is indistinguishable from greenwash; the only response is to make the claims auditable. FRQNCY's own [editorial standards](https://frqncy.network/editorial-standards/) apply the same instinct to curation.

A fund that perfects only the ownership axis is closer to the work than a fund that lightly touches all five. The work is the *practice* of holding the axes in tension, not the slogan that papers them over.

## The map of practitioners — who is actually doing this

Books are slower than the field. The names below are the working layer.

[John Mackey](https://www.consciouscapitalism.org/) and Raj Sisodia codified the *conscious capitalism* operating doctrine through the [Conscious Capitalism](https://frqncy.network/orgs/conscious-capitalism/) organisation — what most operators inside conscious-capital-backed companies actually run on. [Yvon Chouinard](https://frqncy.network/people/yvon-chouinard/)'s Patagonia transfer is the most-cited proof that a multi-billion-dollar company can move into a purpose trust without destroying itself. Marjorie Kelly's work at the Democracy Collaborative on community wealth-building, and the Cleveland Evergreen Cooperatives that came out of it, is the urban-systems analogue.

On the fund side, [Mariana Mazzucato](https://frqncy.network/books/the-value-of-everything/)'s argument for the entrepreneurial state has reshaped the public-investment side of the conversation — the corrective to the assumption that conscious capital is purely private. Sir Ronald Cohen, who chaired the G8 Social Impact Investment Taskforce, is the dean of the institutional impact layer; his *Impact: Reshaping Capitalism to Drive Real Change* (2020) is the standard reference for how state, philanthropy, and private markets interlock at scale. Morgan Simon's *Real Impact* (2017) is the field-practitioner critique from inside — that most "impact" capital still privileges investor return over community outcome. Joel Solomon's Renewal Funds and the Patient Capital Collaborative remain among the older working examples on the patient-capital side. [verify: Cohen, Simon, Solomon are not yet FRQNCY pages.]

The global-South lineage is the necessary complement to the North-American boardroom conversation; any reader who skips it ends up parochial. Muhammad Yunus and Grameen on micro-credit, Bunker Roy and the Barefoot College on demand-side organising, Acumen under Jacqueline Novogratz on patient capital for the global poor — three different theories of how capital compounds welfare. Yunus's *Building Social Business* and Novogratz's *The Blue Sweater* (2009) are the foundational texts.

On the regenerative side, [Paul Hawken](https://frqncy.network/people/paul-hawken/)'s arc — *Natural Capitalism* (1999), *The Ecology of Commerce* (1993), [*Drawdown*](https://frqncy.network/books/drawdown/), and the [Project Drawdown](https://frqncy.network/orgs/project-drawdown/) team — is the longest single-author trajectory in the field. [Carol Sanford](https://frqncy.network/people/carol-sanford/)'s [*The Regenerative Business*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-regenerative-business/) is the firm-level operational manual; [Adrienne Maree Brown](https://frqncy.network/people/adrienne-maree-brown/)'s [*Emergent Strategy*](https://frqncy.network/books/emergent-strategy/) is the relational counterpart — the same instinct applied to coordination and power, not capital flow.

Useful test: practitioners who can name the price they have paid — a deal they walked from, a holding they took a haircut on, a return they did not chase — are in the field. Practitioners who can only name the upsides are in marketing.

## The canon of books — the working shelf

Start with [Raworth, *Doughnut Economics*](https://frqncy.network/books/doughnut-economics/), because the model frames everything downstream; continue with [Meadows, *Thinking in Systems*](https://frqncy.network/books/thinking-in-systems/), because the systems literacy underneath the doughnut is non-negotiable. Read [Mazzucato, *The Value of Everything*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-value-of-everything/) to dismantle the assumption that GDP-as-measured equals value-as-created, and [Hawken et al., *Drawdown*](https://frqncy.network/books/drawdown/) for the working catalogue of climate solutions ranked by impact — the closest the field has to a practitioner's index.

The operating layer follows: [Laloux, *Reinventing Organizations*](https://frqncy.network/books/reinventing-organizations/) for the post-hierarchical company; [Sanford, *The Regenerative Business*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-regenerative-business/) for the regenerative firm; [Chouinard, *Let My People Go Surfing*](https://frqncy.network/books/let-my-people-go-surfing/) for the long-arc owner-founder voice; [Hurst, *The Purpose Economy*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-purpose-economy/) for the workforce side; and [Drucker, *The Essential Drucker*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-essential-drucker/) — the chapters on management as a liberal art and the corporation as a social institution — as the corrective to any reading that imagines this conversation began in 2010.

The substrate next: [McDonough and Braungart, *Cradle to Cradle*](https://frqncy.network/books/cradle-to-cradle/) for the industrial-design grammar; [Benyus, *Biomimicry*](https://frqncy.network/books/biomimicry/) for the design lineage that precedes it; [Kimmerer, *Braiding Sweetgrass*](https://frqncy.network/books/braiding-sweetgrass/) for the indigenous-knowledge correction to the entire framing; and [Ostrom, *Governing the Commons*](https://frqncy.network/books/governing-the-commons/) for the empirical demonstration that the alternatives to market and state are not folklore.

Hold against [Eisenstein, *Sacred Economics*](https://archive.org/details/sacred-economics) for the poetic argument about what money *was* and could be again; against [*The Diamond Cutter*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-diamond-cutter/) for the contemplative counterweight to every fund pitch deck; and against the financial-discipline backbone — [*The Bitcoin Standard*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-bitcoin-standard/) for the monetary substrate, [*Your Money or Your Life*](https://frqncy.network/books/your-money-or-your-life/) for the personal-finance translation — because conscious capital that cannot survive ordinary financial scrutiny is not capital; it is a wish.

Twelve to fifteen books. Six to nine months. After that the next book a reader picks up is the one they should be writing.

## The honest critique — where conscious capital fails

The essay would be hollow without this section. Every serious reader can write at least three critiques; the field is most damaged by the writers who cannot.

**Greenwashing is real and structural.** Most capital that uses the vocabulary of consciousness is doing nothing the prior generation did not do; the language is decorative. The 2022 SEC settlements over misleading ESG claims, the gap between IRIS+ standards and what funds actually publish, and the drift of "impact" labels onto portfolios indistinguishable from index funds are the visible part. The invisible part is the slower drift of the language — vocabulary used without underlying practice loses its capacity to refer.

**The scale problem is real and unresolved.** The global B Corp community in 2026 is still smaller, by market cap, than a single S&P 500 industrial. Patagonia's transfer made headlines because it is rare; Mondragón has stayed Mondragón-sized for sixty years. The field has produced beautiful examples without yet producing a working theory of how it scales without dilution.

**The measurement problem may be intractable.** What conscious capital exists to *do* — keep soil intact, institutions trustworthy, communities better than it found them — does not measure on the time scales of capital. Goodhart's Law applies: every metric instituted has been gamed within a half-decade. The honest position is that the work has to be done while the measurement work is also being done, and that some of the most important outcomes will only become measurable in retrospect.

**The Global North bias is real and persistent.** Literature, funds, certifications, and conferences cluster in the same handful of cities. The Global South lineage — Yunus, Roy, Novogratz on philanthropy; Bolivian, Kenyan, and Indian cooperatives on worker-ownership; Indigenous land trusts everywhere — is regularly cited and rarely treated as the *centre*. A field that takes itself seriously has to relocate its centre of gravity, and so far it has not.

**The "consciousness" problem is the deepest.** The word imports a metaphysical claim the rest of the framework cannot defend on its own terms. Conscious of what — of which costs, on which time horizon, weighed by which lights? Different readers fill in different answers: Buddhist interdependence, Quaker testimony, ecological systems, plain decency. Some of this is generative pluralism; some is the unwillingness to specify a worldview because specifying one narrows the addressable market. The reader has to decide which.

These critiques do not invalidate the project; they specify it. A practitioner who has internalised all five is more useful than a hundred who have not.

## The current frontier — what is now in motion

The field is in motion, and the motion is faster than the books.

Regenerative finance — ReFi — is the most active frontier. The early Toucan / KlimaDAO / Celo carbon-token work in 2021–2022 made the predictable mistakes (low-quality offsets, speculative dynamics that swamped the underlying ecology). The work since 2023 [verify 2026 ReFi-protocol landscape] has been quieter and more rigorous: better verification, longer-dated tokens, on-the-ground partnerships with regenerative farms, watersheds, and Indigenous-stewarded land. The cryptographic substrate gives the field a chance the older impact world could not — micro-denominated, programmable, transparent claims on ecological state. Whether the chance is taken is the question of the next decade.

The doughnut city pilots are the second frontier. Amsterdam adopted the framework as a municipal operating doctrine in 2020; [Doughnut Economics Action Lab](https://doughnuteconomics.org/) has since worked with Brussels, Copenhagen, and smaller jurisdictions. Whether a *city* can be governed to keep itself inside planetary and social boundaries is the question.

The post-growth lineages — Jackson, Raworth, Hickel, degrowth — have moved from heterodox academic conversation to working frame for some sovereign-wealth funds and central banks [verify which as of 2026]. The shift is real; so is the political resistance.

The perpetual-purpose-trust architecture is the third frontier — Patagonia, Newman's Own, the Tata Group, and several large European foundations run versions of the same structure. Legal scholarship is thin; practitioner literature is thinner. The next five years will produce both.

The convergence with network-state and crypto-coordination work is the fourth and least mapped. FRQNCY's [network-state reading list essay](https://frqncy.network/essays/reading-list-network-states/) covers that ground from the polity side; the conscious-capital reading is that the funding model for network states will determine whether they reproduce the failure modes of nation-states or work past them. The same applies to DAOs (see [/dao/](https://frqncy.network/dao/)) — collective governance over shared treasuries is a conscious-capital experiment whether or not the participants call it that.
