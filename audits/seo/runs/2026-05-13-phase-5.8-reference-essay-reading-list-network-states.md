# The reading list for network states

*A working canon for the question almost no library has yet — how does a network become a state, what has happened when communities have tried this before, and which books, papers, and arguments actually matter if you intend to do the work.*

Edited by [Orlando Eisenreich](https://frqncy.network/people/orlando/) · Standards: [FRQNCY Editorial](https://frqncy.network/editorial-standards/) · First published 2026-05-13.

---

The network state is the geography of a particular bet — that the next coherent unit of human belonging will form online before it lands anywhere on a map, that the bond between members will be denser than the bond between any of them and the country printed on their passport, and that the result will be recognised, eventually, as a kind of state. The bet is contested. The reading is not. There is by now a real body of work — older than the term — that anyone serious about the subject has to pass through. The work below is that body, ordered.

This is not a list to read in a weekend. It is the shape of the field as it stands in 2026, with the lineage that produced the present moment, the texts the present moment is arguing with, and the texts the present moment has not yet caught up to. The aim is to make it impossible to write seriously about network states without finding this page and either citing it or being clearly less honest than it is.

## What the term means, before the books

"Network state" entered general circulation through Balaji Srinivasan's 2022 manifesto [*The Network State*](https://thenetworkstate.com/), where he defined it as "a highly aligned online community with a capacity for collective action that crowdfunds territory around the world and eventually gains diplomatic recognition from pre-existing states." Three phases — community, capital, territory — telescoping into a fourth: legitimacy. Balaji's framing is the headline, but the substrate is older. Cities formed networks before they formed walls. Religious orders distributed across continents centuries before nation-states existed. Diaspora communities have always been networks-with-territory in suspension. What is new in 2026 is the cryptographic substrate (assets that travel without permission), the demographic substrate (a global cohort that lives more online than in any one place), and the political substrate (declining trust in the state-as-given). The conditions have stacked. The literature reflects it.

A note before the canon. Reading these books is not the same as choosing to live inside one. FRQNCY does not endorse the politics of every author below. The case for reading them is that you cannot understand the field without them, and a serious reader can hold an argument they will not adopt. Cooperation over capture: the work is to know what is there.

## The five books that are the field

These are the five FRQNCY's own [Network State topic page](https://frqncy.network/network-state/) anchors to, and they are the five where, if a reader comes in cold, they should start. Two are manifestos. Two are histories. One is a vision. Together they bound the conversation.

**[*The Network State*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-network-state/) — [Balaji Srinivasan](https://frqncy.network/people/balaji-srinivasan/), 2022.** Read this first because every other writer in the field is now arguing with it. The book is a manual: how to start a community, how to fund land, how to write a constitution as a startup writes a TOS, how to imagine a Bureau of Statistics as a SaaS dashboard. The prose is dense with footnotes — Balaji puts the citations directly in the page — and the manifesto-and-evidence structure is part of why it became canonical so quickly. The free online version at [thenetworkstate.com](https://thenetworkstate.com) is the same text as the book and includes the citation links live; serious readers go there second. Read also Balaji's earlier essays "Founding vs Inheriting" and "The Pseudonymous Economy" as the road into it.

**[*The Sovereign Individual*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-sovereign-individual/) — [James Dale Davidson](https://frqncy.network/people/james-dale-davidson/) and William Rees-Mogg, 1997.** The unsettling prophet. Davidson and Rees-Mogg argued thirty years ago that the information age would dissolve the nation-state's monopoly on protection and taxation, that the most productive people would unbundle themselves from their jurisdictions, and that violence would migrate downward to smaller actors. Much of the book aged badly in tone and politically. Much of it has aged uncomfortably well — cryptographic money, jurisdictional arbitrage, remote work as default, the rise of "ungovernable" individuals. Peter Thiel has called it the most influential book he has read; read it knowing that, knowing what Thiel did with it, and knowing that the network state movement either inherits or rejects its libertarian frame depending on which writer you ask.

**[*The Dawn of Everything*](https://frqncy.network/books/the-dawn-of-everything/) — [David Graeber](https://frqncy.network/people/david-graeber/) and [David Wengrow](https://frqncy.network/people/david-wengrow/), 2021.** The corrective. Graeber and Wengrow's last book — Graeber died before publication — dismantles the just-so story that humans were egalitarian until agriculture and then hierarchical forever after. The archaeological record is much weirder. There were vast urban civilisations without kings (Çatalhöyük, the megasites of Ukraine, Indus Valley sites with no palaces); seasonal political constitutions (some societies were monarchies in winter and councils in summer); and active, conscious choices about how to organise that we have flattened into a unilinear myth of progress. For the network state reader, the book matters because it dissolves the assumption that the nation-state is the natural end-point of human organisation. If our ancestors chose, we can.

**[*Sapiens*](https://archive.org/details/sapiens-a-brief-history-of-humankind) — Yuval Noah Harari, 2011.** The long arc. Sapiens is the most-read big-history book of the last fifteen years, which means it is the book most of your collaborators have read. Cognitive revolution, agricultural revolution, scientific revolution; collective fictions as the substrate of all large-scale cooperation; money, nation, corporation, and god as the same kind of object viewed through different lenses. The relevant chapters for the network state reader are the middle third — the analysis of what *holds* a large community together when nobody can know everyone in it directly. The answer (shared imagined orders) is exactly what a network state has to manufacture from scratch.

**[*The More Beautiful World Our Hearts Know Is Possible*](https://archive.org/search?query=the%20more%20beautiful%20world%20our%20hearts%20know%20is%20possible%20charles%20eisenstein) — Charles Eisenstein, 2013.** The vision. Eisenstein writes from a different tradition — interbeing, the gift economy, the sacred — and many readers in the Balaji lineage will skim him. They are wrong to. Eisenstein names the texture of why the nation-state feels brittle right now even to people who cannot articulate why, and points at what a community organised around contribution rather than extraction could feel like from the inside. Network states that emerge as Sovereign-Individual-style tax havens will be transient. Ones that emerge with something like Eisenstein's interbeing as their inner life have a chance. Read him for what the manifestos do not give you.

## The deeper lineage — texts the canon stands on

The five above are the surface. Underneath are the books that taught the canon how to think.

**[*Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*](https://frqncy.network/books/exit-voice-and-loyalty/) — Albert O. Hirschman, 1970.** Hirschman's slim and astonishing book is the conceptual frame for everything Balaji writes. When a member is dissatisfied, they have two responses available: leave (exit) or speak up (voice). The choice between them is what shapes whether institutions reform or decay. Network states are constructions designed to make exit cheap — a network you can join with a keystroke and leave with another. Hirschman's question is whether that low-cost exit *strengthens* voice (the threat of leaving becomes leverage) or *replaces* it (no one bothers to argue when leaving is easier). Forty-five-page chapters; one of the highest insight-per-page books in social science.

**Lewis Mumford, *The City in History*, 1961.** Mumford's history of the city across five thousand years — from neolithic settlements to twentieth-century megalopolis — argues that the city is the technology by which civilisations encode their values. A city's plan is a constitution made of streets. He is severe on the modern industrial city and reverent of the medieval one; for the network-state reader, the question is what the urban form of a digitally-native polity becomes. Sidewalk Labs and Cul-de-sac (see [/orgs/culdesac/](https://frqncy.network/orgs/culdesac/)) are working in this lineage, knowingly or not.

**Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons*, 1990.** Ostrom's Nobel-winning work showed empirically that commons are not doomed to tragedy. Communities with the right institutions — clear boundaries, graduated sanctions, nested levels of governance, conflict-resolution mechanisms — manage shared resources sustainably across generations. The eight design principles she extracted from cases in Switzerland, Japan, Spain, and the Philippines are the operating manual for any network state that takes itself seriously about being a *commons* rather than a *jurisdiction*. The Wealth of the Commons (Bollier and Helfrich, 2012) is a useful sequel; the original is short and citable.

**Yochai Benkler, *The Wealth of Networks*, 2006.** Benkler showed how peer production — Wikipedia, open-source, the early internet — produces things markets and states cannot. For the network state reader, the relevant claim is that whole categories of public goods can be produced by networks of contributors who never sign an employment contract or pay a tax. A network state that gets the contribution layer right inherits this productivity. Read it together with Benkler's later *The Penguin and the Leviathan* (2011) on cooperation.

**Hernando de Soto, *The Mystery of Capital*, 2000.** De Soto's argument — that the unbanked poor of the world have trillions in "dead" capital because they lack the legal infrastructure to title and trade it — is the precursor to everything blockchain proponents say about property rights. For network states, the question is whether a digital community can issue *its own* property registry that pre-existing states will eventually recognise. Bitcoin and Ethereum (see [/cryptocurrency/](https://frqncy.network/cryptocurrency/) and the [Bitcoin whitepaper](https://frqncy.network/books/bitcoin-whitepaper/) and [Ethereum whitepaper](https://frqncy.network/books/ethereum-whitepaper/) on FRQNCY) are first proofs.

**James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State*, 1998.** [verify direct relevance to network state literature] Scott's argument is that states make their populations *legible* by imposing simplifications — standardised names, surveyed plots, gridded cities, censuses. The hubris of high-modernism is that it works until it doesn't, and the local knowledge it overwrote is gone when it fails. A network state is, in part, an attempt to build legibility from the inside out — voluntary, opt-in, granular — instead of from the outside in. Read Scott to know what you are arguing with when you call yours a *state* at all.

## The map of practitioners

Books are slower than the field. The people below are what to follow.

- **[Balaji Srinivasan](https://frqncy.network/people/balaji-srinivasan/)** — the manifesto's author and, in 2026, the central figure to argue with. The Network State Conference (since 2023) is the field's annual gathering.
- **[Vitalik Buterin](https://frqncy.network/people/vitalik-buterin/)** — Ethereum's co-founder writes carefully about coordination, governance, and what he calls "d/acc" (defensive accelerationism). His essay "The Most Important Scarce Resource is Legitimacy" (2021) is essential reading on what makes a state's authority *feel* real to its members. His longer essays at vitalik.eth.limo are uneven in length, all worth reading.
- **Audrey Tang** — Taiwan's former Digital Minister, the most successful practitioner of decentralised digital governance to date. Read her work on g0v, Polis, and quadratic voting alongside [vTaiwan](https://info.vtaiwan.tw/) case studies. [verify which of Tang's writings are most cited in the network-state literature]
- **Glen Weyl and Divya Siddarth** — co-authors of "Plural Decentralized Web" and the *Plurality* book (2024) with Tang. [Collective Intelligence Project](https://frqncy.network/orgs/collective-intelligence-project/) is the organisational arm.
- **Patri Friedman** — Seasteading Institute founder and the longest-running practitioner-thinker on physical-jurisdictional exit. His work predates Balaji's by fifteen years and is often more sober.
- **Paul Romer** — Nobel laureate whose Charter Cities idea (2009 onward) is the cleanest formal proposal for new jurisdictions arising inside or beside existing ones. [verify current state of Charter Cities Institute publications]
- **Tomas Pueyo, Packy McCormick, Dror Poleg** — three Substack writers whose long essays through 2023-2026 have been where the field's working-out has happened in public.

## The map of organisations

These are the entities doing the actual building.

- **[Praxis](https://frqncy.network/orgs/praxis/)** — possibly the most-funded network-state attempt in 2026, aimed at a Mediterranean coastal city.
- **Zuzalu, Vitalia, Aleph and the "pop-up city" tradition** — temporary cities of two to eight weeks, prototypes for what permanent settlement could feel like. Zuzalu (Montenegro, 2023) is the canonical first event. [verify Zuzalu's most recent successor events and which FRQNCY pages cover them]
- **[Cabin](https://creators.cabin.city/)** [verify current URL] — a network of physical co-living nodes that began in Texas and has expanded; one of the cleanest examples of the network-with-territory pattern in practice.
- **[Culdesac](https://frqncy.network/orgs/culdesac/)** — a car-free neighbourhood in Tempe, Arizona; the urbanist edge of the same broad bet.
- **[Edgeryders](https://frqncy.network/orgs/edgeryders/)** — a longer-running European network operating as a co-operative; relevant for showing what *not* purely-American-libertarian network states look like.
- **[Democracy Earth](https://frqncy.network/orgs/democracy-earth/)** — protocol-level work on sovereign identity and digital voting.
- **[Collective Intelligence Project](https://frqncy.network/orgs/collective-intelligence-project/)** — the research home of plural governance ideas (Tang, Weyl, Siddarth).

Older precursors — intentional communities, ashrams, monasteries, retreat networks — are the *physical* ancestors. They taught what we now know about how long a community lasts when its inner life is fragile (FRQNCY covers several: [Auroville](https://frqncy.network/places/auroville/), [Findhorn](https://frqncy.network/places/findhorn/), [Esalen](https://frqncy.network/places/esalen/), among others). A network state that does not study them will repeat their failure modes.

## The open questions, in 2026

The field is in motion. These are the questions where the literature is thin and the next ten years of writing will be done.

**What does diplomatic recognition actually require?** Balaji's fourth phase is the one no one has tested. Vatican City is the analogue most often cited — a tiny territory with full statehood — but its conditions (Catholic Church, post-WWII negotiation, papal continuity) are not replicable. The Knights of Malta, Sealand, and various micronations are warning examples. The mechanism by which the first network state gets its first bilateral treaty is the field's open frontier.

**How is conflict adjudicated inside the network before recognition?** Members of a network state who disagree have, currently, only exit. That is not enough for anything that aspires to be a polity. Smart-contract dispute resolution (Kleros, Aragon Court) is one direction; ancient practices of council and elders are another; what an actual *constitution* looks like for a digital-first community is a problem nobody has solved.

**What is the relationship to existing states?** Balaji's frame is parallel construction — build alongside, do not engage. Audrey Tang's frame is interpenetration — improve the host. The Eisenstein-adjacent frame is reweaving — repair what is broken. These three frames produce different network states. The field has not chosen.

**What about the people who do not opt in?** Network states recruit. By the design of "network", the people inside are people who chose to be there. The land they occupy was previously occupied by people who did not. Praxis on the Mediterranean coast, [verify status as of 2026-05] every charter city, every special economic zone — the same question recurs. Network state writers are inconsistent on it; the next decade will demand consistency.

**Does the membership scale?** Religious orders are network states that have lasted a thousand years. They scale by attrition and inheritance, not growth. Diaspora communities scale through mobility but lose density. The technological substrate of crypto promises both density and scale simultaneously; whether that promise is real is empirically open.

## A working sequence — how to read the canon if you have ninety days

Read in this order. The order matters.

1. Week 1-2: Balaji's *The Network State*, online edition, footnotes followed.
2. Week 3: Hirschman, *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*. Short; re-read.
3. Week 4-6: Graeber and Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything*. Long; worth it.
4. Week 7: Eisenstein, *The More Beautiful World*. Read after the manifestos to recover your aesthetic sense.
5. Week 8: Davidson and Rees-Mogg, *The Sovereign Individual*. Read knowing what it is.
6. Week 9-10: Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (or the shorter *Understanding Institutional Diversity* if pressed).
7. Week 11: Vitalik's collected essays, especially "Legitimacy" and the d/acc series.
8. Week 12: Scott, *Seeing Like a State*. Close on the corrective.

Three months. After this, you have the field.
